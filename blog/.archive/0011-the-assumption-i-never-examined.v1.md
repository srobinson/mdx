Thirty years in tech. I trusted the system the way most people trust the weather: not because I'd examined it, but because it hadn't given me a serious reason not to. I built things, shipped things, made money. Capitalism was the ambient condition of doing all of that. I never thought of it as a choice.

The last two years changed the angle, if not the assumption. AI arrived and I found myself genuinely unsettled about what comes next - not personally, but for the structure of things. For societies. For what work means. For what the future looks like for people who didn't land inside a technology cycle at the right moment.

I still didn't frame that as a political question. Then I watched an episode of Jung & Naiv with philosopher Lea Ypi, and something shifted.

Most people I know landed in one of three places when AI arrived.

Camp one: AI solves everything. No one has to work. Abundance for all.

Camp two: AI becomes the instrument of something like a 1984 regime. Totalitarian, surveillance-first, optimized for control.

Camp three: It's sophisticated autocomplete. The capability is overestimated. Nothing fundamental changes.

I've moved between camps two and three depending on the week. But Ypi's question sits outside all three. The camps argue about what AI *can do*. Her question is who controls it and what it gets optimized for. That's a political economy question. None of the three camps are asking it.

I build context and attention infrastructure for AI systems: the plumbing that determines what an agent remembers, what it carries forward, what it attends to. Ypi argues that capitalism tends to commodify cognitive activity, to turn attention and memory into transactions. That lands differently when you spend your days engineering the memory layer for AI agents. A quiet aside, but a close one.

The thing that actually stopped me was a distinction I hadn't made before.

Ypi draws a line between Marxism as a *critique of capitalism* and Soviet communism as an *implementation attempt*. These are separate questions. The implementation failed catastrophically. That doesn't resolve whether the critique holds. Most people I know - including me - treated 1991 as having answered both. It settled what you could say about the implementation. It didn't settle the diagnosis.

I had been carrying a conclusion that hadn't been established. The capitalism assumption: held the way you hold a background fact, not the way you hold a position you've examined. Ypi made it visible as an assumption. That's a different kind of discomfort from being proven wrong.

I am not converted. The alternative isn't compelling either.

That's what I didn't expect. I assumed that questioning the assumption would leave me somewhere else: a different camp, a different certainty. Instead there's a space without ground. Every authority that might tell me what to think has a visible agenda. The economists who defend capitalism have funders. The critics have frameworks they're selling. The institutions that should hold the middle have spent decades compromising it.

The binary feels like a trap. Capitalism or communism, pick one. Anything outside that frame gets treated as naive.

The sadness is specific. I haven't concluded capitalism is wrong. The comfort of not having examined the question is just gone now, and that doesn't come back.

Something is being decided. The decision isn't in any public forum. It's being made by whoever ends up controlling the intelligence layer: the infrastructure that determines how AI systems are built, what they optimize for, whose interests they serve.

The concentration of AI capability in a small number of organizations, all of them with fiduciary obligations pointing in a particular direction - this isn't the neutral background condition it presents itself as. It's a choice being made while most of the conversation stays focused on capability.

What does a society look like when the cognitive infrastructure is owned by whom? What does daily life look like when the intelligence layer is a utility, a product, or something we don't have a name for yet? What gets optimized for when the people doing the optimizing answer to shareholders?

I build AI cognitive infrastructure. I started paying attention to who builds it for society, and what assumptions they're carrying while they do.

Every token counts.
