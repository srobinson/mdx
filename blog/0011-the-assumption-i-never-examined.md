Thirty years in tech. I trusted the system the way most people trust the weather: not because I'd examined it, but because it hadn't given me a serious reason not to. I built things, shipped things, made money. Capitalism was the ambient condition of doing all of that. I never thought of it as a choice.

That's worth sitting with. Thirty years is a long time to carry an assumption without noticing it. I'm not making a dramatic confession. The assumption wasn't ideological in any conscious sense. I didn't wake up each morning committed to the free market. I hadn't read Friedman or Hayek or anyone else who might have given me a framework to defend if pressed. The assumption was more basic than that: the system produced value, rewarded effort across reasonable timescales, and let me build a life. That felt like enough. The water was warm enough that I never thought to ask what it was made of.

What that looked like in practice: thirty years of shipping software, building teams, navigating technology cycles. I watched the internet arrive and reshape commerce, watched mobile do it again, watched cloud computing change the economics of everything downstream. Each wave created opportunities for people who could move inside it. I was one of those people. The companies I worked with hired, grew, sometimes went public. The products reached users who seemed to benefit from them, or at least chose to use them. I had no serious grievance with the system. Why would I? It had been, from where I was standing, an almost unbroken series of confirmations.

The last two years changed the angle, if not the assumption. AI arrived in a way that felt qualitatively different from the previous waves, and I found myself genuinely unsettled about what comes next - not personally, but for the structure of things. For societies. For what work means to people who didn't land inside a technology cycle at the right moment. The scale of the capability shift felt like something that affects everyone, not just the people building or using the tools. I started paying attention to a different kind of question.

I still didn't frame that as a political question. Then I watched an episode of Jung & Naiv with philosopher Lea Ypi, and something shifted.

---

When AI arrived in the public conversation, most people I know sorted themselves into one of three camps. The sorting felt fast and instinctive, as if the camp had been waiting to be occupied.

Camp one: AI solves everything. The scarcity problems that have plagued human civilisation become engineering problems with known solution paths. No one has to work unless they want to. Abundance, democratised. This camp tends to skew toward people who have spent careers building software and watched each previous wave of technology produce more than it destroyed.

Camp two: AI becomes the instrument of something resembling what Orwell described. Totalitarian, surveillance-first, optimised for the maintenance of power. The capability is real; the question is only who gets to it first and what they do with it. This camp tends to produce the most urgent writing.

Camp three: the capability is overestimated. Large language models are sophisticated pattern matchers. The systems produce impressive-looking outputs but don't generalise in the ways that matter. Nothing fundamental is changing. This camp tends to skew toward people who have looked closely at the technical architecture and found reasons for restraint.

I've moved between camps two and three depending on the week, depending on what I'd read most recently, depending on whether I was watching a demo or thinking about the underlying systems.

But Ypi's question sits outside all three. The camps argue about what AI *can do*: capability, risk, ceiling. Her question is who controls it and what it gets optimised for. The two questions feel related but they're actually orthogonal. You can believe AI will be enormously capable and still not have asked who decides what it's for, under what incentive structure, accountable to whom. That is a political economy question. None of the three camps were asking it, and I hadn't been either.

---

Before I get to what she said, I want to explain why I listened to her specifically.

Lea Ypi is the Ralph Miliband Professor of Politics at the London School of Economics. She was born in 1979 in Tirana, Albania, and grew up under Enver Hoxha's regime: the most isolated and ideologically rigid of the Eastern Bloc states. Hoxha broke with the Soviets in 1961, with Mao's China in 1978, and ran Albania as a sealed laboratory of Stalinism until his death in 1985. Religion was banned in 1967. Private property was effectively abolished. The country was studded with concrete bunkers built against an invasion that never came. There were no cars visible on the streets. The economy was commanded by a party that tolerated no dissent and had severed almost every connection with the outside world.

Ypi was eleven when the regime collapsed in December 1990. What followed is central to understanding her argument. Albania was declared an IMF success story of free-market reform by 1995. Two years later, in 1997, the country detonated. Pyramid schemes - into which the population had poured savings after being told this was capitalism working - collapsed with liabilities equal to roughly half of GDP. Civil order dissolved. Two thousand people died. The army deserted. The national armouries were looted. Ypi watched all of this from close range, as a teenager in a country that had just been told the free market would save it.

She left, eventually, for Italy and then the UK. She completed a PhD at the European University Institute in Florence, arrived at LSE in 2009, and was elected a Fellow of the British Academy in 2024. Her 2021 memoir, *Free: Coming of Age at the End of History*, covers the Albanian years and was translated into more than twenty languages. She is a working Kant scholar whose academic articles engage the technical literature of analytic and continental political philosophy. She is not a polemicist.

That biography matters. The standard rhetorical move against anyone who questions capitalism is: go live in the USSR. Ypi already did, under conditions considerably worse than most Western critics can imagine. She has also watched a transition to capitalism collapse into pyramid-scheme chaos on a national scale. She has no idealised version of either system, because she has seen both fail real people in sequence, in the same country, within a decade of each other.

Her argument in the Jung & Naiv conversation runs like this: democracy and capitalism are structurally incompatible. Not occasionally in tension, not manageable with better regulation - structurally incompatible at the level of what each system requires to function. Capitalism, defined as private ownership of the means of production organised around profit, generates what she calls structural domination: not bad behaviour by individual capitalists, but impersonal, systemic pressures that channel everyone's choices through market imperatives. Democracy in any meaningful sense requires that citizens stand as equals capable of authoring the rules under which they live. Structural domination cuts the legs from under this. The vote is real. But it operates inside an economic order whose basic terms are not on the ballot.

I had never heard the argument put quite that way. Not because I had examined it and rejected it - because it had never crossed my path in a form I could engage with.

---

The thing that actually stopped me was a distinction I hadn't made before.

Ypi draws a line between Marxism as a *critique of capitalism* and Soviet communism as an *implementation attempt*. I had been treating these as the same thing. Most people I know do. The Soviet Union failed; Marxism failed. Case closed. 1991 settled it.

The problem is that Marx wrote almost nothing about how a post-capitalist society should actually be administered. Three volumes of *Capital* analysing the internal mechanics of capitalist production - its laws of motion, why it generates recurring crises, why capital concentrates over time. And then, on the constructive side, scattered passages amounting to a few dozen pages. The *Critique of the Gotha Programme* is the most developed document. It is fifteen pages.

The USSR was built on theoretical innovations Lenin and his successors introduced and that Marx did not endorse: party vanguardism, democratic centralism, a planned economy administered by a single-party state with secret police. Arguing that the Soviet collapse refutes Marx's analysis of capital is roughly equivalent to pointing at the Spanish Inquisition to refute the Sermon on the Mount. The critique ("capitalism alienates labour, concentrates capital, commodifies social life") and the implementation ("five-year plans run by a single party with secret police") are different propositions. Evaluating one tells you something about the other, but it does not settle it.

What 1991 actually demonstrated is worth separating out. It proved that centrally planned economies of the Soviet type cannot match modern market economies for productivity, innovation, or consumer welfare. Hayek was largely right about the calculation problem: prices aggregate dispersed, tacit, local knowledge in ways a central planner's model cannot replicate. One-party states are not stable, attractive, or just.

What 1991 did not demonstrate: that Marx's structural analysis of capital, crisis, and alienation was wrong. That liberal-democratic capitalism is internally stable. Within thirty years of that moment we have had a global financial crisis, a sovereign debt crisis in Europe, the rise of authoritarian capitalism in China and Russia, populist revolts across the Western democracies, runaway wealth concentration, and a climate emergency. Fukuyama himself walked his end-of-history thesis back substantially. His 2022 book is titled *Liberalism and Its Discontents*.

The 1991 frame did real work in the 1990s. It is doing less work now, and the parts of the global landscape it cannot explain are the ones that matter most.

I had been carrying a conclusion that hadn't been established. The capitalism assumption: held the way you hold a background fact about physics, not the way you hold a position you've examined and found defensible. Ypi made it visible as an assumption. That is a different kind of discomfort from being proven wrong.

---

A quiet aside, since it's close enough to name.

I build context and attention infrastructure for AI systems: the plumbing that determines what an agent remembers, what it carries forward across a conversation, what it attends to and what it drops. The work is about making AI agents less amnesiac, more capable of genuine continuity. This is the layer beneath the visible outputs - the infrastructure that shapes what the system can know and hold.

Ypi follows Marx in arguing that capitalism tends to commodify cognitive activity. Attention, memory, knowledge, care: each gets progressively pulled into the logic of exchange, priced, transacted, extracted. This is not a moralistic complaint about consumerism. It is a structural claim about what happens to social relations when more and more of life is mediated by market exchange.

Marx identified four forms of alienation that capitalist wage labour produces. Alienation from the product of your labour, which is owned by someone else and confronts you as something foreign. Alienation from the activity of work itself, directed and organised for another's profit rather than your own flourishing. Alienation from what he called species-being: the human capacity for free, conscious, purposive creation, reduced to a means of survival. And alienation from other people, relationships mediated by wages and commodities rather than mutual recognition.

Crucially, alienation in this framework is not a feeling. A worker can love their job and still be alienated in this technical sense. The condition is structural, not subjective. The separation is real whether or not it is felt.

When I spend my days engineering the memory and attention layer for AI agents, and then encounter an argument that the commodification of human cognitive activity is a central feature of the system I've been operating inside for thirty years - the two things knock together. I'm not sure what to do with that. But it's close enough to be worth naming.

---

Something I didn't expect: I am not converted. The alternative isn't compelling either.

I had assumed that questioning the assumption would leave me somewhere else. A different certainty. A different camp. Instead there is a space without ground. Every authority that might tell me what to think has a visible agenda.

The economists who defend capitalism have funders, departments, careers embedded in institutions the system built. The critics have frameworks they're selling, political traditions they're defending, identities bound up with the diagnosis. The journalists who cover this produce content optimised for engagement on platforms that have turned attention into their primary product. The think tanks are funded by people with a stake in the outcome.

This is not cynicism. It is an observation that the credibility signal I had been relying on - trust the educated, credentialed, published consensus - doesn't point in a clear direction here. The serious, credentialed, published people are on every side. And many of the institutions that should hold the middle ground have spent decades making compromises that made the middle ground harder to stand on.

The binary itself feels like a trap. Capitalism or communism, pick one. Anything outside that frame gets treated as naive or insufficiently serious. But "socialism" is not a single thing. It is a family of positions that ranges from Nordic social democracy - which accepts capitalism as the underlying economic system and builds a welfare state on top - to democratic socialism, which holds that the economic structure itself needs transformation, not just better regulation. These are meaningfully different proposals. The word "socialism" has been doing the work of collapsing that distinction for decades, and the collapse serves particular interests.

The sadness is specific. I haven't concluded capitalism is wrong. The comfort of not having examined the question is just gone now. The assumption that the system I'd operated in for thirty years was correct because it had worked for me - I can now see that this was a conflation. A system working for me and a system being the best available option for everyone are different claims. I had not noticed I was running them together.

That comfort doesn't come back. But the space it leaves is not nothing. It is where a real question lives.

The structural inequality argument, which I had understood as a theoretical point about the long run, has become empirical in the last eighteen months. Productivity gains from AI in knowledge work are flowing to organisations and shareholders. The workers whose output those gains touch are not seeing those gains converted into wages. This is not a distant prediction about manufacturing jobs in a future decade. It is happening now in legal work, in finance, in software, in writing - in the sector I have spent thirty years working in. The Marxist critique says the hierarchy is structural, not incidental. What is happening in 2025 and 2026 looks more like a structural outcome than an incidental one.

---

Something is being decided. The decision is not in any public forum. It is being made by whoever ends up controlling the intelligence layer: the infrastructure that determines how AI systems are built, what they optimise for, whose interests they serve.

A small number of organisations hold most of the relevant capability right now. These organisations have fiduciary obligations pointing in a particular direction, and investors with expectations about returns. They operate inside a capitalist structure not because anyone chose it as the right structure for building the next layer of human cognitive infrastructure, but because that is the water they are swimming in. The same water I have been swimming in for thirty years.

Branko Milanovic, an economist who works on inequality and is not given to Marxist framings, describes the present moment as a competition between two capitalisms: liberal meritocratic capitalism, anchored in the United States and Western Europe, and political state-led capitalism, anchored in China. In his analysis, the next serious ideological conflict will not be between capitalism and socialism. It will be between these two variants, with different mixes of private ownership, state direction, and accountability to citizens.

In January 2025, DeepSeek - a Chinese AI lab - released a model competitive with the best American ones, built at a reported fraction of the compute cost. American AI-related stocks shed hundreds of billions in valuation in a single session. The event made Milanovic's framing concrete in a way no amount of argument had. The two capitalisms are already competing at the intelligence layer. The race is live, and the outcome is not determined by the starting positions.

If that framing is correct, the question of who controls the intelligence layer is also the defining geopolitical question. Not in a distant or abstract sense. In the sense that the decisions being made right now - about which organisations build the systems, under what governance structures, optimised for whose benefit - are decisions with long tails. The infrastructure that gets built shapes what becomes possible.

There is a harder question underneath this. AI data centers are among the fastest-growing consumers of electricity on the planet right now. The infrastructure being built to process and generate knowledge runs on energy at a scale that is showing up in national power grid planning documents. Nancy Fraser, extending Marx, describes capitalism as cannibalising its own background conditions: the ecological systems, the care work, the public goods it depends on but does not account for in any price. The energy math for AI is one version of that problem playing out in real time. The infrastructure being built to solve things is adding to the bill it was supposed to reduce.

What does a society look like when the cognitive infrastructure is owned by whom? What does daily life look like when the intelligence layer is a utility, a product, or something we don't have a name for yet? What gets optimised for when the people doing the optimising answer to shareholders? And if the alternative is the state-led model - the other available capitalism - what gets optimised for then, and by whom?

I don't have answers. I had a fifty-three-year-old's settled certainty about the economic question, and one philosopher's hour-long interview dissolved it. What I walked away with was not a new certainty. It was a different kind of attention - to a question I hadn't known I wasn't asking.

We are at a junction. Most of us did not choose to be here at this particular moment. The decisions about what the road ahead looks like are being made by a small number of people, under incentive structures the rest of us didn't set, inside a political economy that wasn't designed for this. I find I have more compassion for all of us in it - for the people building the systems, who are also swimming in the water; for the people whose work is being reshaped by what the systems can now do; for the people trying to think clearly about it in real time without adequate frameworks; for the people who aren't thinking about it at all yet, which is most people, and who are nonetheless going to live inside whatever gets decided.

I build AI cognitive infrastructure. I started paying attention to who builds it for society, and what assumptions they're carrying while they do.

Every token counts.
