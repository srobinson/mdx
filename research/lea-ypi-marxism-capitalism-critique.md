## Executive Summary

Lea Ypi is the most-read political philosopher of her generation arguing that the failures of state socialism do not vindicate capitalism, and that the failures of capitalism do not vindicate state socialism. Her authority rests on a rare combination: she lived under one of the most brutal Stalinist regimes in Europe (Albania under Enver Hoxha) as a child, watched it disintegrate into pyramid-scheme chaos in the 1990s, and is now Ralph Miliband Professor of Politics at LSE working on Kant, Marx, and the Austro-Marxist tradition.

The reader's instinct that "capitalism is better" was shaped by a 1991 frame that has been doing more work than it can bear. The collapse of the USSR proved a great deal about Soviet command planning and the Leninist party-state. It proved much less about Marx's analysis of capital, about democratic socialism, or about whether the present global order can sustain itself. The serious debate today is not capitalism-versus-Soviet-tyranny. It is whether liberal-democratic capitalism is internally stable and morally defensible, with intelligent people arguing both sides.

## 1. Lea Ypi: The Person and Why She Is Credible

### Biography

Born 1979 in Tirana, Albania. Grew up under Enver Hoxha's regime, the most isolated and ideologically rigid of the Eastern Bloc states. Hoxha broke with Yugoslavia in 1948, with the USSR in 1961, and with Maoist China in 1978, leaving Albania a sealed laboratory of Stalinism until his death in 1985. Religion was banned in 1967. Private property was effectively abolished. The country was studded with concrete bunkers built against an invasion that never came.

Ypi was eleven in December 1990 when the regime collapsed. The seven years that followed are central to her argument. Albania was declared an IMF "success story of free market reforms" in 1995. Two years later, in 1997, the country detonated. Pyramid schemes with liabilities equal to roughly half of GDP imploded; the population had poured savings into them after being told this was capitalism. Civil order disintegrated, two thousand people died, the army deserted, and the national armouries were looted. Ypi watched this firsthand.

She left for Italy and then the UK, took a PhD at the European University Institute in Florence, and has been at LSE since 2009.

### Academic position

- Ralph Miliband Professor of Politics at the London School of Economics. (Miliband was a foundational New Left Marxist; the chair signals where the department places her.)
- Fellow of the British Academy, elected 2024.
- Research areas: normative political theory, Kant and the Enlightenment, Marxism and critical theory, migration and territorial rights, Balkan intellectual history.
- Earlier book: *Global Justice and Avant-Garde Political Agency* (Oxford, 2012), a scholarly work attempting to reconcile cosmopolitan moral principles with associative obligations of states.
- 2025 book: *Indignity: A Life Reimagined*, a follow-up that uses her grandmother's life through Ottoman collapse and Albanian communism to interrogate fact, fiction, dignity, and historical memory.

### What she actually argues (the Jung & Naiv frame)

Ypi's headline claim in conversations like J&N #794 is direct: **democracy and capitalism are not compatible.** Unpacked, this is not a slogan but a structural argument with three moves:

1. *Capitalism* is defined as a social structure characterised by private ownership of the means of production and the pursuit of profit. This is a definition, not an insult.
2. Such a structure produces what she calls *structural domination*: not bad behaviour by individual capitalists, but impersonal, systemic constraints that channel everyone's choices through market imperatives.
3. *Democracy* in any robust sense requires that citizens stand as moral equals capable of authoring the rules under which they live. Structural domination cuts the legs out from this. The vote is real but it operates inside an economic order whose basic terms are not on the ballot.

Her positive proposal is **moral socialism**: a Kantian-inflected reconstruction of the socialist tradition, drawing on the Austro-Marxists of the early 20th century (Otto Bauer, Max Adler, Karl Renner) and on the German Neo-Kantians. The core normative engine is Kant's second formulation of the categorical imperative, "treat humanity never merely as a means but always as an end." Ypi argues that this maxim cannot be realised in a society organised around the extraction of surplus from labour and the commodification of social life. Moral socialism is therefore a critique of capitalism *and* of state socialism, both of which she reads as instrumentalising persons.

### Why she is taken seriously

Three reasons that an undecided reader should care:

1. **Empirical immunity.** The standard rhetorical move against socialists is "go live in the USSR." Ypi already did, with significantly more deprivation than most Western critics could imagine. She is not romanticising what she has not seen.
2. **Theoretical grounding.** She is a working Kant scholar. Her academic articles ("On Revelation in Kant and Marx," "On Revolution in Kant and Marx," her PhD on "Statist Cosmopolitanism") engage the technical literature of analytic and continental political philosophy. She is not a polemicist.
3. **Productive distance from the camps.** The Marxist orthodoxy reads her as too Kantian and too liberal. Liberal critics read her as too Marxist. This is usually the position from which interesting work emerges.

## 2. Conceptual Distinctions Most People Get Wrong

### Marxism vs. "actually existing socialism"

The single most common confusion: treating Marx's *critique of capitalism* as if it were a *blueprint* whose implementations we can audit. It is mostly the former.

- Marx wrote three volumes of *Capital* analysing how capitalist production works, what laws of motion it follows, and why he expected it to generate recurring crises and concentrations of capital. He wrote almost nothing about how a post-capitalist society should be administered. The *Critique of the Gotha Programme* and a few scattered passages are the entire constructive corpus.
- The USSR, Mao's China, Hoxha's Albania, Pol Pot's Cambodia, Castro's Cuba: each was an attempt by 20th-century revolutionaries to seize state power and impose a transition to socialism in conditions Marx explicitly said were not ripe (largely agrarian economies, before bourgeois capitalism had matured). Lenin and his successors made theoretical innovations (party vanguardism, democratic centralism, planned economy) that Marx did not endorse and, in many cases, would have rejected.
- Pointing at Soviet famine, Stalinist purges, or Albanian bunkers to refute *Capital* is a category error roughly equivalent to pointing at the Spanish Inquisition to refute the Sermon on the Mount. The diagnosis ("capitalism alienates labour, concentrates capital, commodifies social life") and the implementation ("five-year plans run by a single party with secret police") are different propositions and need to be evaluated separately.
- This is not a Marxist apologetic. Even some critics of Marx grant the distinction. The economist Steve Paxton's 2024 book *Unlearning Marx* argues that the Soviet failure was, on Marx's own categories, a vindication: a state-bureaucratic mode of exploitation is exactly what Marx's framework would predict if you tried to skip the bourgeois stage and impose collective ownership before the productive forces were ready.

### Soviet/Maoist communism vs. Marxism vs. socialism

A useful three-layer model:

- **Marxism**: a research programme in social theory and a critique of capitalism. Continuously developed since 1867 by hundreds of scholars (Lukács, Gramsci, the Frankfurt School, Althusser, the analytical Marxists, Harvey, Fraser).
- **Communism**, lower-case, in Marx's sense: the hypothetical post-capitalist society of free producers. Capital-C **Communism** in 20th-century usage: the political movement organised around the Third International (Comintern) and its national parties, identified with Leninist-Stalinist regimes.
- **Socialism**: a much older and broader tradition that long predates Marx (Saint-Simon, Owen, Fourier, Proudhon) and that includes non-Marxist branches (Christian socialism, guild socialism, market socialism, social democracy). Socialism is a *family* of positions about democratising economic life, not a single doctrine.

Treating "socialism" and "Soviet communism" as synonyms collapses three centuries of debate into one bumper sticker.

### Democratic socialism vs. social democracy

Frequently used interchangeably in casual speech. They are not the same thing.

- **Social democracy** (think postwar Sweden, Germany, the UK Labour Party from Attlee to Blair): accepts capitalism as the underlying economic system, but builds a robust welfare state, strong labour protections, progressive taxation, and public provision of health, education, housing, and infrastructure. The means of production remain mostly privately owned. The goal is a *fair* capitalism, not a different system. Social democracy is reformist.
- **Democratic socialism** (think early Eugene Debs, Michael Harrison, the Democratic Socialists of America in their stronger statements, much of Bernie Sanders's youthful framing): holds that capitalism is structurally incompatible with democracy and equality, and that democratic political institutions should be used to transform the underlying economy toward worker self-management, public or cooperative ownership of major means of production, and democratised investment. Democratic socialism is transformational.

Practical overlap exists at the policy level (both will support universal healthcare, sectoral bargaining, expanded public services) and the labels are often used loosely. The structural disagreement is whether you are *managing* capitalism humanely or *replacing* it democratically.

Ypi sits closer to the democratic-socialist end. Piketty in his recent work calls his position "participatory socialism" and locates it explicitly past the limits of social democracy. Bernie Sanders in policy practice is closer to social democracy. The terminology matters because it tracks whether the speaker thinks the basic structure of the economy is up for democratic decision.

### Socialism as a spectrum

A more useful taxonomy than left-vs-right:

| Variant | Means of production | Allocation | Political form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soviet command socialism | State-owned | Central plan | One-party state |
| Yugoslav market socialism | Worker-managed firms | Market | One-party (Tito-era) |
| Nordic social democracy | Privately owned, regulated | Market + welfare state | Liberal democracy |
| Democratic socialism (DSA-type) | Mixed: cooperatives, public, private | Market + planning | Liberal democracy + workplace democracy |
| Ypi's moral socialism | Underspecified, oriented toward worker control and decommodification | Market with serious limits on commodification | Democratic, deliberative |
| Piketty's participatory socialism | Codetermination, capped ownership | Market with confiscatory wealth/inheritance taxes | Liberal democracy |
| Hägglund's democratic socialism | Collectively owned | Plan oriented to reducing socially necessary labour time | Democratic |
| Anarcho-syndicalism / libertarian socialism | Worker collectives, federations | Market or gift economy | Stateless |

The single word "socialism" covers all of these. A reader who believed "capitalism beat socialism" was usually told a story in which "socialism" meant only column 1.

## 3. The Marxist Critique of Capitalism: The Strongest Version

The strongest version is not "rich people are mean." It is structural.

### Alienation of labour

From the *1844 Manuscripts*. Marx identifies four mutually reinforcing forms of estrangement that capitalist wage-labour produces:

1. From the *product*: what you make is owned by someone else and confronts you as something foreign.
2. From the *activity* of work: the work itself is not an expression of your capacities; it is coerced, directed by another, organised for profit not flourishing.
3. From your *species-being* (Gattungswesen): the human capacity for free, conscious, purposive activity is reduced to a means of survival.
4. From *other people*: relationships at work and in the wider economy are mediated by the wage and the commodity, not by mutual recognition.

Crucially, alienation in Marx is not a feeling. It is an objective structural condition. A worker can love their job and still be alienated in this technical sense. That distinction is what gives the concept teeth and what most pop-culture summaries miss.

### Commodification of social life

Marx's analysis of the commodity form (Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1) argues that under capitalism, social relations between people increasingly take the form of relations between things. Labour, time, attention, care, education, healthcare, housing, even genetic material and personal data: each is progressively pulled into the form of commodities priced and exchanged in markets. This is not a moralistic complaint about consumerism. It is a claim that the *logic* of social reproduction changes when more and more of life is mediated by exchange.

Nancy Fraser's contemporary update (*Cannibal Capitalism*, 2022) extends this with the metaphor of capitalism as cannibalising its own background conditions: care work, ecological systems, public goods, and democratic politics are progressively commodified or starved, even though the economic system depends on them. Her four "boundary struggles" (production/reproduction, society/nature, economy/polity, exploitation/expropriation) are a useful map of where structural critiques are pressing in 2025.

### Structural inequality vs. incidental inequality

This is the critical distinction.

- **Incidental inequality**: some people are richer than others because of luck, talent, effort, or circumstance. Liberalism takes this seriously and proposes corrections via taxes, transfers, equal opportunity policy.
- **Structural inequality**: the economic order *requires* a class of people who must sell their labour to a class of people who own the means by which labour is set to work. The hierarchy is not a deviation from the system; it *is* the system. Cleaning up the edges through redistribution does not change the underlying relation.

Marxists argue capitalism produces structural inequality necessarily. Liberals argue capitalism produces incidental inequality contingently, and that good policy can largely fix it. Most of the modern debate (Rawls vs. G. A. Cohen; Piketty vs. mainstream growth economists; Ypi vs. her liberal critics) sits on this fault line.

Piketty's empirical contribution since *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014) has been to show that when r > g (rate of return on capital exceeds growth rate of the economy), wealth concentration is the default trajectory and you need active political-tax intervention to prevent oligarchy. This is not a Marxist claim per se; Piketty was for years carefully not-a-Marxist. By *Capital and Ideology* (2020) and *Time for Socialism* (2021), he has moved measurably leftward and now uses the s-word.

### "Capitalism won in 1991": what it actually proved

Sober inventory of what the Soviet collapse demonstrated:

**It did prove:**
- Centrally planned economies of the Soviet type cannot match modern market economies for productivity, innovation, and consumer welfare. Hayek and Mises were largely vindicated on the calculation problem.
- One-party states with secret police are not stable, attractive, or just.
- "Catch-up industrialisation under a vanguard party" is a dead road.
- Marxist-Leninist legitimacy stories cannot survive when the population can compare their living standards with West Germany's.

**It did not prove:**
- That Marx's analysis of capital, crisis, alienation, and commodification was wrong. These are claims about how capitalism works internally, not about whether the USSR worked.
- That liberal-democratic capitalism is internally stable. Within thirty years of 1991 we have had: the 2008 financial crisis, a sovereign debt crisis in Europe, the rise of authoritarian capitalism in China and Russia, populist revolts in the US and Western Europe, runaway concentration of wealth, and a climate emergency.
- That all alternatives have been tried. Worker cooperatives, market socialism with public capital allocation, codetermination, sovereign wealth funds, participatory budgeting, sectoral bargaining, public banking: most of these have been tested only at small scale or in particular sectors. The 20th century ran one experiment (Soviet command planning) very loudly and a few others (Yugoslavia, Mondragon, Nordic codetermination) more quietly.
- That the absence of a serious rival means the present order is correct. Fukuyama himself walked his thesis back substantially. By his own later admission (*Liberalism and Its Discontents*, 2022), liberalism faces serious internal threats.

The 1991 frame did real work in the 1990s. It is doing less work now, and the parts of the global landscape it cannot explain (China's success, Western democratic decay, climate, inequality, the resurgence of socialist politics in countries it was supposed to have buried) are the ones that matter most.

## 4. The Strongest Defenses of Capitalism

Strawmen are easy. The serious arguments are these.

### Hayek's epistemic argument

Friedrich Hayek's central claim, developed in "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945) and *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960), is not that capitalists deserve their gains. It is that **markets are an information system**. Prices aggregate the dispersed, tacit, local knowledge of millions of actors in a way no central planner can replicate. Any attempt to plan production centrally must throw away this information and substitute the planner's much-poorer knowledge. The Soviet collapse is, on this view, an empirical confirmation.

The serious socialist response, since Lange-Lerner in the 1930s and now mechanism design theory and computational economics, is that markets are not the *only* information system, and that hybrid institutions can preserve much of the price signal while changing ownership. But Hayek's argument is real, and any serious socialist has to engage it.

### Friedman's freedom argument

Milton Friedman, *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962): economic freedom is necessary, though not sufficient, for political freedom. The voluntary exchange of goods and labour decentralises power. State control of the economy concentrates the means of life in the hands of whoever runs the state, which is a precondition for tyranny. Empirically, most political freedoms have emerged inside capitalist economies; few have survived in centrally planned ones.

The socialist response is twofold: (a) the freedom to spend your wage is hollow if the wage itself is set by structural pressures you cannot influence, and (b) economic concentration in private hands is also a precondition for tyranny, just a private one. Ypi's specific move is to reframe freedom in Kantian terms: not negative non-interference but positive moral autonomy, which capitalism is in tension with. But Friedman's worry about state concentration is real, and the 20th-century evidence on this is not flattering to centralised socialism.

### McCloskey's "Great Enrichment" argument

Deirdre McCloskey's *Bourgeois* trilogy (2006-2016): the period since roughly 1800 has produced an unprecedented increase in human living standards, on the order of 30-100x per capita real income in much of the world. Whatever capitalism's faults, this is the largest welfare improvement in history, and it happened under broadly bourgeois-commercial institutions and a culture that began to dignify trade and innovation. Ideas and norms, not just capital accumulation or imperial extraction, drove the change.

The socialist response is partly empirical (much of the gain is concentrated, much was extracted from colonies, the ecological bill is now coming due) and partly conceptual (correlation across two centuries is consistent with several causal stories, and the *post-1980 neoliberal* phase is a different beast from the broader Great Enrichment). But the basic fact of the Great Enrichment is real, and any serious anti-capitalism has to explain why a future system would do better, not just feel better.

### Cowen's institutional-pragmatic argument

Tyler Cowen's *Big Business* (2019) and his "state capacity libertarianism" argue that real-existing big firms in real-existing market democracies, despite specific abuses, produce broadly trustworthy goods and services, treat workers better on average than the alternatives, and generate the productive capacity that even the welfare state depends on. Anti-capitalist sentiment, on Cowen's diagnosis, often misreads ordinary commercial life as more sinister than it is.

This is the moderate, technocratic defense of the status quo with reforms. It is not a defense of laissez-faire fundamentalism. It accepts a substantial regulatory and welfare state.

### Where Ypi's critics push back

Specific objections raised against her in academic and public reviews:

- The "liberal utopia" she dismisses is not actually utopian. Canada, much of Western Europe, parts of East Asia have produced large, functioning, redistributive liberal democracies. Calling them "still waiting to be realised" is rhetorically cheap.
- "Actually existing socialism" exhibited the very vices she attributes to capitalism (selfishness, careerism, exploitation by nomenklatura). Why should we expect a renewed socialism to escape this?
- Her argument that a future socialism could be different than the Albanian past is, by her own framing, unverified. Critics in the *LSE Review of Books* note that this leaves justice and freedom as something like an "unrealisable dream," which is awkward for a tradition (Marxism) that prides itself on materialist analysis.
- Liberal egalitarians (Rawlsians) argue that her structural-domination critique of capitalism does not require socialism in any strong sense; a "property-owning democracy" or "liberal socialism" of the kind Rawls himself sketched might address it without breaking from market institutions.

The critic who matters most is probably the rigorous liberal egalitarian who says: *we agree on most of the diagnosis; we disagree that the cure must be socialism rather than a much more redistributive and democratised capitalism.*

## 5. Live Intellectual Fault Lines (2025)

The serious debates today are not capitalism-vs-USSR. They are these.

### Is liberal-democratic capitalism internally stable?

Wolfgang Streeck (*How Will Capitalism End?*, 2016, with a 2024 reissue): the postwar marriage of democracy and capitalism was a contingent compromise that is unravelling. Five disorders: declining growth, oligarchy, starved public sphere, corruption, international anarchy. No agent capable of reform.

Branko Milanovic (*Capitalism, Alone*, 2019): capitalism is now the only game, but in two competing forms: liberal meritocratic (US, Western Europe) and political/state-led (China, Russia, parts of the developing world). The next ideological conflict is *between* capitalisms, not between capitalism and socialism. Liberal capitalism's vulnerabilities are assortative mating, intergenerational wealth transmission, and a shrinking middle class.

Fukuyama himself (*Liberalism and Its Discontents*, 2022): liberal democracy is genuinely under siege from both populist right and identitarian left, and the threats are largely internal.

This is now mainstream centrist analysis, not Marxist.

### Where does the moral case sit?

G. A. Cohen, the Oxford analytical Marxist, argued in *Why Not Socialism?* (2009) and *If You're an Egalitarian, How Come You're So Rich?* (2000) that the moral case for socialism stands on three pillars (community, equality, reciprocity) and that the burden is on capitalism to show why these should be abandoned at the factory gate. Cohen's work is the most accessible serious academic-philosophical case for socialism on Anglo-American terms.

Martin Hägglund's *This Life* (2019) reframes the case in terms of secular faith, finitude, and freedom. We have one life, and it is wasted by labouring under conditions we did not choose; democratic socialism is the political form that lets us spend that life on what matters. This is the most-discussed recent philosophical defense of socialism in the Anglosphere.

Ypi sits in this neighbourhood, but with a Kantian engine rather than Hägglund's Hegelian one. The two are in dialogue (see their published *Philosopher* magazine conversation, 2021).

### Can socialism actually be calculated?

The Mises-Hayek calculation problem (1920s-1940s) is not dead. It is the strongest single argument against any non-market socialism, and modern socialists must answer it. Recent answers:

- Computational/mechanism-design socialism (Cottrell, Cockshott, more recently AI-augmented planning proposals): post-Cold War increases in computing power change the feasibility analysis.
- Market socialism (Roemer, Schweickart): keep markets for goods and services, socialise capital. Worker cooperatives compete, but rents on capital flow to a public fund.
- Participatory economics (Albert, Hahnel) and other post-market designs: less convincing to most economists.

Most serious contemporary socialists (Piketty, Wright, the Jacobin/New Left Review milieu) accept that markets in goods and services are here to stay, and concentrate the fight on ownership, capital allocation, and decommodification of specific spheres (healthcare, housing, education, care work).

### What about climate?

This is the new pivot. The "second contradiction of capitalism" thesis (originally James O'Connor, now Fraser, Jason Moore, Andreas Malm, Kohei Saito): capitalism's own logic of accumulation is incompatible with the ecological limits of the planet, and no amount of green-tech reform can resolve this without a fundamental change in the property and accumulation regime.

Ecomodernists and climate-liberals reject this and argue technology + carbon pricing can decouple growth from emissions.

This debate is now arguably the live frontier; it is where the most interesting younger thinkers sit (Saito's *Capital in the Anthropocene* sold over 500,000 copies in Japan and was a global hit on translation).

### Where the serious thinkers sit (a partial map)

| Position | Key voices |
|---|---|
| Defenders of capitalism (sophisticated) | Tyler Cowen, Deirdre McCloskey, Fukuyama (qualified), Sam Bowman/centrist neoliberalism, the *Economist* editorial line |
| Reformist liberal egalitarian | John Rawls (legacy), Elizabeth Anderson, Martin O'Neill, Stuart White (property-owning democracy) |
| Social-democratic / participatory-socialist | Thomas Piketty, Branko Milanovic (descriptive more than normative), Wolfgang Streeck |
| Democratic socialist | Lea Ypi, Martin Hägglund, Erik Olin Wright (deceased 2019, still influential), Jacobin/Catalyst contributors |
| Marxist (academic) | David Harvey, Nancy Fraser, Slavoj Žižek, Michael Roberts, Kohei Saito, John Bellamy Foster |
| Heterodox right (post-liberal) | Patrick Deneen, Adrian Vermeule, integralists; this is the *other* serious anti-capitalism on the cultural right |

The reader should know that the post-liberal right is also in the anti-capitalist game now. Deneen and the integralists also argue liberal capitalism corrodes community, family, and meaning. Ypi and Deneen agree on more of the diagnosis than either would publicly admit; they part company entirely on the cure.

## 8. Open Questions for Further Investigation

1. What does Ypi's positive moral-socialist programme look like at the level of institutions (ownership, allocation, governance)? She is more articulate about what she opposes than what she proposes. *Free* and *Indignity* are memoirs; the constructive theory is in scattered articles.
2. How does her position relate technically to Cohen's analytical Marxism? Both are Anglo-academic, both Kant-influenced, both democratic. There is an unfilled comparison there.
3. Where does the *Indignity* (2025) project fit into her larger philosophical arc? Reviews suggest it is less directly political than *Free* and more concerned with truth, memory, and dignity.
4. What is the strongest *empirical* recent rebuttal to the "capitalism is structurally incompatible with democracy" thesis? Defenders point to the postwar Western European experience; critics point to the 2008-onwards trajectory. The honest answer is contested.

## 9. Actionable Takeaways for the Reader

1. **Decouple "Marx critiques capitalism" from "the USSR was bad."** These are different claims and can be evaluated independently. Granting the second does not refute the first.
2. **Treat "socialism" as a family of positions, not a single doctrine.** The taxonomy in section 2 is a tool you can use whenever someone says "socialism failed." Ask: which kind?
3. **Read the strongest defenders of capitalism, not the strawmen.** Hayek's epistemic argument and McCloskey's Great Enrichment argument deserve to be wrestled with. Skipping them leaves the conviction soft.
4. **Read the strongest critics on their own terms.** Ypi's *Free*, Cohen's *Why Not Socialism?*, Hägglund's *This Life*, and Piketty's *Time for Socialism* are the four most accessible serious entry points. Picking one of the four and reading it cover-to-cover will move you further than any number of podcasts.
5. **Notice the post-liberal right.** Anti-capitalism is no longer the left's monopoly. Deneen's *Why Liberalism Failed* (2018) makes parallel structural critiques from a Catholic-conservative direction. Engaging with it sharpens the question of which features of the present order are genuinely worth defending.
6. **Watch the climate frontier.** The most serious 2020s anti-capitalist work (Saito, Malm, Fraser) is increasingly framed in ecological terms. If the climate-incompatibility argument is right, the political stakes shift dramatically.
7. **Resist the temptation to settle the question.** The honest position after this scaffolding is not "I've changed my mind" but "I now see this is a real debate among serious people, and the 1991 'capitalism won' narrative is not adequate to it." That is a more durable and intellectually honest place to land than picking a new team.
