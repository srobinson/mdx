# Rendering Coplanar Surfaces in Real-Time 3D

## 1. Depth-buffer precision and z-fighting

**Definition.** Z-fighting ("stitching"/"planefighting") occurs when two or more primitives have near-identical camera-space distance, so their stored depth values round to the same or adjacent quantized buffer value; the depth test then can't reliably order them, and the winner flickers per-pixel/per-frame. [Wikipedia: Z-fighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-fighting)

**Root cause 1 — quantization.** The depth buffer stores a fixed number of bits (commonly 24-bit fixed-point, or 16/32-bit) per fragment. Two fragments whose true depths differ by less than one quantization step map to the same stored value, so the depth test cannot distinguish them. This is unavoidable at *some* distance; it just needs to be pushed out of the space the scene occupies. [Wikipedia: Z-fighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-fighting)

**Root cause 2 — non-linear (1/z) mapping from perspective projection.** For a standard OpenGL-style perspective projection, clip-space `w = -z_eye`, and the post-divide NDC/window depth `d` is an affine function of `1/z`:

```
d ≡ z_clip / w_clip = (a + b·z) / z = a·(1/z) + b
```

(Nathan Reed derives this directly from the projection matrix's third row.) Because `d` is affine in `1/z`, not in `z`, depth values are packed extremely densely near the far plane and spread out near the near plane. This 1/z relationship is *also* what makes depth linear in **screen space** across a triangle (it's why hardware can interpolate it with simple barycentric/DDA math and why hierarchical-Z / early-Z optimizations work) — Emil Persson's point, cited in Reed's article. [Nathan Reed, "Depth Precision Visualized"](https://www.reedbeta.com/blog/depth-precision-visualized/)

**Near/far ratio dominates precision.** Reducing the near-plane distance pushes the usable `d` range toward the asymptote of the 1/z curve, concentrating nearly all resolvable precision within a small distance of the camera and starving everything beyond it — this is the single biggest lever on precision, far more than the far-plane value (pushing the far plane to infinity has comparatively little effect). Practical guidance: keep `near` as large as the scene allows, since precision loss scales with `far/near`, not `far - near`. [Nathan Reed, "Depth Precision Visualized"](https://www.reedbeta.com/blog/depth-precision-visualized/) — also covered under "why depth precision is nonuniform" in the Khronos OpenGL wiki's Depth Buffer Precision article (moved to wikis.khronos.org/opengl/Depth_Buffer_Precision; direct fetch returned 403 during this research pass, but the article is the standard secondary citation alongside Reed's post and is linked from Reed's own references).

**Coplanar faces are the degenerate case.** For two polygons that are mathematically coplanar, the interpolated depth *should* be identical at every pixel — there is no finite offset that quantization needs to resolve, only floating-point/rasterizer rounding noise deciding the winner per-fragment. This is why coplanar z-fighting is chaotic/flickering rather than a stable "wrong but consistent" ordering: it's arbitrary rounding noise, not a systematic precision shortfall. [Wikipedia: Z-fighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-fighting)

## 2. Reversed-Z

**What it is.** Reversed-Z maps the near plane to depth `1.0` and the far plane to depth `0.0` (the opposite of the conventional 0-near/1-far mapping), combined with a **floating-point** depth buffer. Because IEEE float has exponentially more mantissa precision clustered near zero, and the reversed mapping puts the *far* plane at the depth-value region where float already has poor absolute precision (near 0, but relative precision stays high) while the *near* plane sits at 1.0, the composition of "1/z depth curve" and "float's own exponential precision distribution" very nearly cancels out — yielding a close-to-uniform distribution of resolvable precision across the whole `[near, far]` range. [Nathan Reed, "Depth Precision Visualized"](https://www.reedbeta.com/blog/depth-precision-visualized/)

**Reed's measured result:** with reversed-Z + float32 depth, his depth-comparison-error simulation showed a **0% error rate**, versus a **45% indistinguishability rate** for a conventional (non-reversed) setup at the same scene parameters. His conclusion is unambiguous: *"In any perspective projection situation, just use a floating-point depth buffer with reversed-Z!"* [Nathan Reed, "Depth Precision Visualized"](https://www.reedbeta.com/blog/depth-precision-visualized/)

**Requirements to apply it.** Reversed-Z needs two things the API must support together:
1. A floating-point depth attachment (`depth32float` or similar) — an 8-bit-exponent float depth buffer is what gives the precision win; reversed-Z with a plain fixed-point depth buffer is pointless/harmful.
2. Control over the clip-space-to-NDC depth range mapping (or an equivalent flip), because you need `near → 1, far → 0` instead of the API default. In OpenGL/WebGL this is `glClipControl`/`EXT_clip_control` setting the depth mode to `ZERO_TO_ONE` (matching D3D/Vulkan/WebGPU conventions) combined with a flipped projection matrix and an inverted depth-compare function (`GL_GEQUAL`/`"greater"` instead of `GL_LEQUAL`/`"less"`). [Khronos WebGL Extension Registry: EXT_clip_control](https://registry.khronos.org/webgl/extensions/EXT_clip_control/) (direct fetch 403'd in this pass; confirmed via search-result excerpt and cross-referenced against the analogous desktop `ARB_clip_control`/`EXT_clip_control` OpenGL registry entries)

**Status across APIs / three.js, verified July 2026 (three.js r185, released 2026-07-01):**

| API / Renderer | Reversed-Z status | Notes |
|---|---|---|

Maintainer framing (Mugen87, three.js core team, on the discourse thread above): reversed-Z is meant to fully replace logarithmic depth buffer once stable, because it preserves early-Z / early depth-test hardware optimizations that logarithmic depth necessarily disables — *"If you have reverse-z, logarithmic depth buffer is obsolete."* [three.js discourse thread](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/does-three-js-webgpu-support-reverse-z-buffer/87687)

**Reversed-Z and coplanar faces specifically:** reversed-Z is a precision redistribution technique, not a tie-breaker. It reduces the *rate* at which nearby-but-distinct depths collide, but for polygons that are exactly coplanar it changes nothing — both faces still map to literally the same depth value (just a different-but-still-identical one), so the flicker persists. It does not substitute for polygon offset / render-order techniques for true coplanarity (see §3, §4).

## 3. three.js `logarithmicDepthBuffer`

**Mechanism.** With `WebGLRenderer({ logarithmicDepthBuffer: true })`, three.js computes a logarithmic depth value per-vertex and writes it explicitly in the fragment shader via `gl_FragDepth` (using the `EXT_frag_depth` extension on WebGL1; native in WebGL2/GLSL ES 3.00), rather than letting the rasterizer's linear interpolation of clip-space `z/w` stand. Source (three.js `dev` branch, `src/renderers/shaders/ShaderChunk/`):

- Vertex: `vFragDepth = 1.0 + gl_Position.w;` (also records `vIsPerspective`)
- Vertex clip-space z, per the original technique (Outerra / three.js formula):
  `gl_Position.z = log2(max(1e-6, 1.0 + gl_Position.w)) * Fcoef - 1.0;` where `Fcoef = 2.0 / log2(farPlane + 1.0)`
- Fragment: `gl_FragDepth = vIsPerspective == 0.0 ? gl_FragCoord.z : log2(vFragDepth) * logDepthBufFC * 0.5;`

[three.js source, `logdepthbuf_fragment.glsl.js` / `logdepthbuf_vertex.glsl.js`, `dev` branch](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/src/renderers/shaders/ShaderChunk/logdepthbuf_fragment.glsl.js); technique originates from Outerra's planet-scale renderer work: [Outerra blog, "Logarithmic depth buffer optimizations & fixes" (2013)](https://outerra.blogspot.com/2013/07/logarithmic-depth-buffer-optimizations.html) — this post is also where the `log2`-instead-of-`ln` optimization and the `max(1e-6, …)` near-camera-plane-crossing fix originate. It was originally added to three.js in [PR #3880, "Logarithmic depth buffer support"](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/3880).

**Cost.** Writing `gl_FragDepth` explicitly forces per-fragment depth computation in the shader, which **disables early-Z / hierarchical-Z hardware rejection** for that draw, because the GPU can no longer trust the rasterizer-interpolated depth to reject occluded fragments before running the fragment shader. Confirmed pitfall reports: heavy overdraw scenes (thousands of overlapping sprites/foliage) see a measurable frame-rate hit with `logarithmicDepthBuffer` enabled, and antialiasing along intersecting/coincident-edge geometry misbehaves because MSAA depth resolves assume rasterizer-computed depth. [three.js discourse: "Beware of logarithmic depth buffer--it can degrade scene performance!"](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/beware-of-logarithmic-depth-buffer-it-can-degrade-scene-performance/88495); [Issue #17384, "Suggestion: Remove use of gl_FragDepthEXT for Logarithmic Depth Buffer"](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/17384); [Issue #22017, "Antialiasing has artifacts when logarithmic depth buffer is enabled"](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/22017)

**Does it help true coplanar faces? No — verified.** Logarithmic depth buffering redistributes precision across the near/far range so that distinct-but-close depths (e.g. a huge terrain plus nearby detail objects) don't collide. It does nothing for two faces that are mathematically identical in depth: both encode to the exact same `log2(vFragDepth)` value, so the "which wins" question is still resolved by rasterization/floating-point rounding noise, same as with a linear depth buffer. This is stated generally for all depth-encoding schemes (linear, logarithmic, reversed-Z alike): none of them are a tie-breaker for coincident geometry — cross-referenced from the general z-fighting literature: "polygon offset is a useful tool when you intend to render coplanar or near-coplanar geometry... the remedy is to either offset one [surface] slightly in depth during rendering, merge aligned geometries, or use a higher-precision depth buffer" — the higher-precision-buffer option is explicitly scoped to *near-coplanar*, not exactly-coplanar, geometry. [Wikipedia: Z-fighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-fighting); general treatment in [Medium, "Practical Analysis on the Z-fighting and the Logarithmic Depth Tests for Computer Graphics"](https://medium.com/@e92rodbearings/practical-analysis-on-the-z-fighting-and-the-logarithmic-depth-tests-for-computer-graphics-43509504e065)

**Bottom line for coplanar surfaces:** neither logarithmic depth buffer nor reversed-Z is the right tool. Both only fight quantization/rounding *between distinct depths*. Coplanar faces need an explicit geometric or render-order separation (§4, §5).

## 4. Polygon offset / depth bias

**OpenGL semantics (`glPolygonOffset(factor, units)`).** Applies only when polygon-offset mode is enabled for the current raster mode (`GL_POLYGON_OFFSET_FILL`, `_LINE`, or `_POINT`). The offset is added to each fragment's depth value *after* the rasterizer interpolates it from the polygon's vertex depths, and *before* the depth test / depth-buffer write. The offset is:

```
offset = factor · DZ + r · units
```

- **`DZ`** — the maximum depth slope of the polygon: how steeply depth changes with respect to window-space x/y across the triangle. A polygon face-on to the camera has `DZ ≈ 0`; a polygon at a glancing/grazing angle has a large `DZ`, because a tiny change in screen position corresponds to a large change in depth. `factor` scales this term, so `factor` automatically compensates for polygon slope — it applies *more* offset to steeply-angled polygons and *less* to face-on ones.
- **`r`** — an implementation-defined constant: the smallest depth-buffer increment guaranteed to produce a distinguishable depth value (roughly "one quantization step" of the depth buffer, though the exact value is implementation-specific and not required to be exposed). `units` scales this fixed, slope-independent term.

[Khronos OpenGL 4 Reference Pages: `glPolygonOffset`](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glPolygonOffset.xhtml) (direct fetch 403'd for this specific page during this pass — cross-confirmed via [docs.gl `glPolygonOffset` (GL ES 3)](https://docs.gl/es3/glPolygonOffset), which mirrors the Khronos reference-page wording verbatim, and the [OpenGL Wiki "Parameters of Polygon Offset"](https://wikis.khronos.org/opengl/Parameters_of_Polygon_Offset) which walks the `DZ`/`r` derivation in more depth (khronos wiki blocked 403 on direct fetch in this pass, referenced via search-indexed excerpt)).

**Practical implication of the two-term formula:** `factor` is what you tune for *silhouette/grazing-angle robustness* (coplanar faces viewed edge-on need proportionally more push to stay separated), while `units` is what guarantees a *minimum, angle-independent* separation even for perfectly face-on coplanar polygons where `DZ = 0` and `factor` alone would contribute nothing. Typical practice (also reflected in the OpenGL Programming Guide's canonical example, see §6) is `factor = 1, units = 1` as a starting point, then increasing `units` if flicker persists at silhouette-heavy viewing angles, since a nonzero `factor` term vanishes exactly at `DZ = 0`.

**three.js exposure.** `Material.polygonOffset` (boolean, default `false`), `Material.polygonOffsetFactor` (number, default `0`), `Material.polygonOffsetUnits` (number, default `0`) map 1:1 onto `gl.polygonOffset(factor, units)` plus enabling `GL_POLYGON_OFFSET_FILL`. Source doc comment: *"Whether to use polygon offset or not. When enabled, each fragment's depth value will be offset after it is interpolated from the depth values of the appropriate vertices."* [three.js source, `src/materials/Material.js`, `dev` branch](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/src/materials/Material.js); public docs: [`Material.polygonOffset`](https://threejs.org/docs/#api/en/materials/Material.polygonOffset)

**Typical values in three.js usage:** for "edge lines over a solid face" or "decal over base surface" cases, small integer-ish values are standard — e.g. `polygonOffsetFactor: 1, polygonOffsetUnits: 1` on the *back* (base) material to push it away from the camera relative to the un-offset overlay, or negative values (`-1, -1`) applied to the overlay to pull it toward the camera instead. Either direction works since offset is signed; the point is to break the tie deterministically in screen space rather than relying on scene-space geometry separation.

**Pitfalls:**
1. **Applies per-material/per-draw-call, not per-pixel-relationship.** The offset is a property of the material used for a whole mesh's draw call — you can't selectively offset only the pixels that happen to be coincident with another specific mesh; you offset the whole surface uniformly.
2. **Does not affect raycasting/picking.** three.js's `Raycaster` operates on the actual triangle geometry in object/world space; `polygonOffset` is a GPU rasterization-and-depth-test-only bias applied downstream of the geometry, so ray-triangle intersection math never sees it. A raycast against two offset-separated coplanar meshes can therefore disagree with what's visually on top.
3. **Glancing-angle artifacts.** Because `factor · DZ` grows with slope, at near-grazing viewing angles the effective offset can become large enough to visibly detach the offset surface from the base surface (visible gap/peeking-through), or in the other direction, insufficient if `units` alone is too small — this is the standard failure mode reported in three.js forum threads on the topic. [three.js discourse: "The polygon offset is not correct when viewed from the front"](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/the-polygon-offset-is-not-correct-when-viewed-from-the-front/57631)
4. **`r` (the units scale) is implementation/hardware-dependent**, so a `polygonOffsetUnits` value tuned on one GPU/depth-buffer-format is not guaranteed to be exactly equivalent on another; treat it as "needs enough headroom," not an exact physical unit.

## 5. Alternative coplanar techniques

| Technique | Mechanism | When it's the right call |
|---|---|---|
| **`depthFunc` LEQUAL/EQUAL second pass** | Render the base surface normally (writes depth), then render the coincident overlay with `depthFunc = GL_LEQUAL` (three.js: `material.depthFunc = THREE.LessEqualDepth`, which is actually three.js's *default* depth function already — the default `LessEqualDepth` rather than strict `LessDepth` exists specifically so a second draw at the *same* depth is guaranteed to pass rather than losing to floating-point noise) and `depthWrite = false` so it doesn't corrupt the depth buffer for further coplanar draws. Ordering between multiple LEQUAL-tested overlays then becomes purely a function of draw order. | Cheap, no geometry change, good for "always show this decal/highlight on top of its coincident base" where draw order is controllable and you don't need offset in world space. |
| **Stencil-based decals / coplanar polygon masking** | Classic SIGGRAPH technique: draw the base polygon normally (depth + color + stencil no-op), then draw the decal polygon with stencil configured so it can only affect pixels the base already touched, and disable/relax the depth test for the decal pass (since it's coincident) — the stencil mask, not the depth buffer, prevents the decal from bleeding onto occluding geometry. Rationale explicitly stated in the source: *"When one polygon is rendered directly on top of another, the depth buffer cannot be relied upon due to quantization... The stencil buffer solves this by ensuring that the decal polygon can only affect pixels that were touched by the base polygon."* | Needed when polygon offset's uniform push isn't acceptable (e.g. the decal must be pixel-exact with zero visible gap and no glancing-angle drift), and when other geometry may partially occlude both the base and the decal — the stencil test correctly handles that occlusion where a naive offset-and-redraw does not. Source: [SIGGRAPH 2000 Advanced OpenGL course notes §3.5, "Coplanar Polygons and Decaling with Stencil"](http://www.bluevoid.com/opengl/sig00/advanced00/notes/node21.html) — page did not resolve via direct fetch in this research pass (TLS handshake failure on the legacy host); cross-confirmed via search-indexed excerpt of the same page and consistent with three.js's own `DecalGeometry` note that stencil is the traditional coplanar-decal solution. |
| **`renderOrder` + `depthTest`/`depthWrite` manipulation (three.js-specific)** | `Object3D.renderOrder` only changes the **sequence in which three.js issues draw calls** within the same render pass/material-transparency bucket — it does not touch the depth buffer's semantics. If both objects still have `depthTest = true` and `depthWrite = true`, the GPU's depth test still runs exactly as normal after each draw, so reordering alone does not guarantee the later-drawn object wins at coincident pixels (it can still lose the depth test to whatever the earlier draw already wrote, especially non-deterministically for exact ties). The pattern that actually forces "later renderOrder always visually wins" is `renderOrder` (to control draw sequence) **combined with** `depthTest = false` on the object that must always show, so it paints over the earlier draw unconditionally, or with `depthWrite = false` on both, letting the last GPU write to the color buffer settle every coincident pixel while the depth buffer is not being used to arbitrate at all. | Cheap UI-style overlays (gizmos, selection highlights, "always on top" annotations) where you explicitly do not want depth-buffer arbitration; wrong tool for physically-embedded coplanar geometry that still needs to be depth-occluded by *other, non-coincident* scene geometry, since disabling `depthTest` also removes correct occlusion against everything else. |
| **Camera-space "shrink toward camera" offset** | Instead of a screen-space depth bias, nudge the overlay geometry's vertices a small, fixed distance along the camera-to-vertex vector (or along the face normal) in view/world space before the projection, so the depth difference is encoded geometrically rather than via `glPolygonOffset`'s post-rasterization bias. | Useful when you need the separation to survive raycasting (unlike `polygonOffset`, since it's real geometry) or when you want a per-vertex-controllable, non-uniform offset (e.g. thicker near silhouettes) rather than the single flat/slope-scaled bias `glPolygonOffset` provides. |

## 6. The classic "wireframe/edges over solid" problem

Polygon offset's canonical origin use case, per the OpenGL Programming Guide ("Red Book"), Chapter 6 ("Blending, Antialiasing, Fog, and Polygon Offset"), is exactly hidden-line rendering of a solid mesh's silhouette/crease edges: draw the object twice — once as filled polygons in the polygon's fill color with polygon offset enabled to push it slightly *away* from the eye, and once as `GL_LINE`-mode (wireframe) polygons at the true, un-offset depth, in the edge-highlight color. Because the filled pass is nudged back by at least one resolvable depth unit, the wireframe pass wins the depth test everywhere an edge coincides with the interior of a filled polygon, giving clean hidden-line-style edge overlay without a separate line-visibility algorithm. The guide notes polygon offset is "usually the preferred technique, since polygon offset is almost always faster than [the equivalent] stencil buffer[-based]" approach for this specific problem. [OpenGL Programming Guide (Red Book), Ch. 6, summarized at "Hidden-Line Removal (OpenGL Programming)"](http://what-when-how.com/opengl-programming-guide/hidden-line-removal-opengl-programming/) (direct fetch failed with a connection error to the host during this pass; content cross-confirmed via search-indexed excerpt quoting the guide directly, including the "polyoff" example program name from the official Red Book example repository).

**Modern alternatives to the same problem** (silhouette/crease rendering over a solid), for completeness:
- **`THREE.LineSegments` from `EdgesGeometry`/`WireframeGeometry` + `polygonOffset` on the solid material** — the direct three.js-idiomatic descendant of the Red Book technique: offset the *solid* mesh's material away from camera (`polygonOffsetFactor`/`Units` > 0) rather than trying to offset the thin line geometry, since lines have no meaningful `DZ` slope of their own.
- **Screen-space/post-process outline techniques** (depth+normal edge detection in a post-process pass, or inverted-hull silhouette rendering) sidestep polygon offset and z-fighting entirely by not relying on depth-buffer coincidence at all — the outline is derived from a discontinuity in a G-buffer rather than drawn as literally coplanar/coincident geometry.
